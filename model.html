How the model works

The whole method in plain English: what goes in, how a score comes out, and how the picks are kept honest.

The short version

Every Division I team (all 365 of them) gets a rating from four things it does on offense and the same four things it allows on defense. The rating is adjusted for how strong its opponents have been. The gap between two teams’ ratings, plus about 4 points for the home team, becomes a projected margin and a projected score. Every pick is posted before tip-off, locked, and graded against the final score where anyone can see it.

It began as a spreadsheet. The same formulas now run automatically every morning.

Step 1: Rate every team from four things

Basketball games are decided by a short list of things: making shots, keeping the ball, rebounding your own misses and making free throws. Coaches and analysts call them the four factors. The model scores each team on all four, for its own offense and for what its opponents manage against its defense, and weights them like this:

FactorWeightOn offenseOn defense
Shooting40%field goal %opponents’ field goal %
Turnovers25%share of possessions not turned overturnovers forced
Rebounding20%own misses reboundedopponents’ misses kept off the glass
Free throws15%free throw %opponents’ free throw %

A turnover rate is turnovers divided by the possessions that could have ended in one (shots, trips to the line and turnovers). A rebound rate is offensive rebounds divided by the misses that were there to get. The four offense scores add up to an offense rating, the four defense scores to a defense rating, and the two together are the team’s rating.

Every team page lists these eight numbers with their national ranks, so you can see exactly why a team rates where it does. Start from the Rankings.

Step 2: Adjust for who they played

Shooting 50% against the weakest schedule in the country is not the same as doing it in a major conference. So the model measures how strong each team’s opponents have been, using nothing but game results: who beat whom, by how much, and where. Margins are capped at 25 points so running up the score does not count, and home court is taken out. A team that has played a tough schedule gets credit for it in its rating, and a team that has feasted on weak opponents is docked.

Step 3: Early in the season, last year fades out

Three games tell you very little about a team. So each season starts from last season’s rating, and this season’s numbers take over a little more with every game: after 5 games a quarter of the rating is new, after 10 games half, and from game 20 on last season is gone. Right now the typical team has played 6 games, so about 30% of a typical rating is this season and 70% is last season.

Last season’s rating also leans twice as hard on how strong a team’s schedule was, because the league a team plays in says more about next year’s team than last year’s shooting numbers do. A team that is new to Division I has no last season at all, so it starts near the bottom and earns its way up.

Step 4: From ratings to a score

The gap between two ratings is turned into points: one full point of rating is worth 170 points of margin, a scale that was fitted to more than 2,400 real games. The home team then gets 4 points, which is what home teams have actually been worth this season. Nobody gets it at a neutral site.

The projected total comes from how many points each team’s games usually produce: the average of what it scores and what it allows, added for the two teams. Half the total goes to each side, the favorite gets half the margin added and the underdog has it taken away. The first-half pick is the same side, leading at the break by 55% of the projected final margin.

Worked example

Butler vs Nebraska, Nov 22

  1. Ratings: Butler 1.0528, Nebraska 1.1019. Nebraska is ahead by 0.0491.
  2. Into points: 0.0491 × 170 = 8.3 points better than Butler on a neutral floor.
  3. Home court: Neutral floor, so nobody gets a home-court edge.
  4. Projected margin: Nebraska by 8.3.
  5. Projected total: the two teams’ scoring levels add up to 151.1 points.
  6. Projected score: half the total each, plus and minus half the margin: Butler 71, Nebraska 80.
  7. What happened: Nebraska won 76–74. The pick was right, and the margin missed by 7 points.

What the labels mean

A strong pick is a game the model projects by 15 points or more. An upset pick is one where the model picks the team the betting line has as the underdog, and an ultra upset is an upset pick that is also a strong pick. An O/U play is a game where the projected total is at least 10 points away from the posted over/under.

Best Bets

The Best Bets page asks a different question: not who wins, but where the model sees a better chance than the price implies. Betting lines are sharp, so the model does not replace them. It first puts its own margins on the same scale as that day’s spreads, then starts from the line and moves 35% of the way toward its own number, and works out the chance of each bet winning from how much real games scatter around a projection (about 11 points for margins and 16 for totals).

A bet makes the list only if the model gives it at least a 55% chance and that chance beats what the odds imply by 3 percentage points or more. Some bets are never taken: spreads of 10 points or more (early in the season the model underrates blowouts), moneyline favorites shorter than −250, and any game where the model and the line are more than 20 points apart, because that usually means bad data rather than a bargain. Each game can put only its best bet on the list.

The day’s top 6 single bets are locked once most of the day’s games have a line, and they are graded won, lost or push from the final score like every other pick. The game behind the top bet is the Game of the day. Parlays built from the same bets are listed too; a parlay needs every leg to win, so it loses far more often than a single bet.

Locked, graded, never edited

A pick only means something if it cannot be changed afterward. Each pick is written to a public record the moment it is posted, with the time. No pick is added for a game that has already tipped off, the betting line saved with a pick is the first one seen that day, and results are graded from the final score, usually the same night. Wrong picks stay on the record next to the right ones.

Through Nov 22, 2026 the model has picked the winner in 702 of 929 games (76%), and in 318 of 336 strong picks (95%). The honest way to read a model is by how sure it was, so here is the same record split by the size of the projected margin:

What the model does not know

It sees box scores and results, and nothing else. It does not know that a star is injured or suspended, that a team is on the last night of a road trip, or that a coach is resting starters. It does not watch games. It also knows less in November than in February, when every team has a full season of its own numbers. The betting market knows all of those things, which is why the model is at its weakest when it disagrees with the line.

It keeps learning

Every final score goes back into the ratings the next morning. The settings are checked against results too: the home-court value, for example, was raised from 2.7 to 4 points in mid-November 2026 after two weeks of games showed home teams doing better than the model expected. When a setting changes, picks already on the record stay exactly as they were posted.

Questions people ask

When are the picks posted?

Each day’s picks are posted overnight, by about 4:30 AM Central, and the page is refreshed before each block of tip-offs through the day. A game added to the schedule late gets its pick at the next refresh, as long as it has not tipped off.

Does a pick ever change after it is posted?

No. A pick is locked the moment it is posted. If the ratings move later in the day, the posted pick stays as it was, and that is the one that gets graded.

What is the early look?

Once the day’s last game has tipped off, the Today page lists the next day’s games with projected scores. Those are not picks yet: the official picks are made overnight, after the night’s results are counted, and can differ slightly.

Why is a game missing?

The model only rates Division I teams, so a game against a lower-division opponent gets no pick. A bracket game whose opponent is not decided yet appears once the matchup is set.

Why does the model like a team the betting line does not?

The line knows things the model does not, such as injuries and lineup news, and it is usually closer to the truth. When the two disagree the game is marked as an upset pick so you can see it for what it is: the riskiest kind of pick on the site.

Is this betting advice?

No. It is a model’s opinion, published so anyone can check it against the results. It is wrong often, as the record shows, and nothing here should be read as a reason to bet money.

Is it free?

Yes. There is no sign-up, no paid tier and no picks for sale.

Words used on this site

Spread: the number of points the betting line gives the underdog. Moneyline (ML): a bet on who wins, with no points. Over/under (O/U) or total: the combined points of both teams. Edge: the model’s chance of a bet winning minus the chance its odds imply. Push: a bet that lands exactly on the number and is refunded. Neutral site: a game where neither team is at home.
